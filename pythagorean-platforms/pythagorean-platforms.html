Pythagorean Platforms

A jungle game about the Pythagorean theorem. Each canyon is a right triangle: leg a is the distance straight across to the next platform, leg b is how high the vine's anchor is above you, and the hypotenuse c is your rope, all measured in feet. Solve one step of the Pythagorean theorem at a time by choosing the correct line of the written solution (tap it, or press the left or right arrow key); each correct step swings you to the next platform, and a wrong step drops you in the quicksand with a hint. The last step is the big swing: time slows down for a close-up that labels all three sides and reveals the missing length, and you land one rope length below the anchor, so short ropes keep you high in the trees and long ropes swing you low. World 1 asks one question per canyon: count the 1-foot squares along the edge of the square on the rope and type the rope length. World 2 walks through every step with tile squares, World 3 is a timed challenge that starts by counting the squares along one side with whole-number sides, and World 4 finds a missing leg, the distance to the next platform, from numbers carved on a temple door. World 5 mixes bigger triangles and has students type the missing number in each line of the solution.

Pythagorean Platforms

How to play

  • One step per swing. Tap the next line of the written solution (or press the left or right arrow key). The right line swings you to the next platform.
  • Steps: pick the formula, plug in the legs, square, add, take the square root.
  • Each canyon is a right triangle: a is the distance across to the next platform, b is how high the vine's anchor is above you, and c is your rope. Find the rope length or the distance to the next platform. All lengths are in feet.
  • When the problem is solved, time slows down for a close-up of all three sides, and then you take the big swing. The next platform is one rope length under the anchor, so a short rope keeps you high in the trees and a long rope swings you low.
  • A wrong line drops you in the quicksand, shows a hint, and you try that step again.
  • World Map: press the map button or Esc to pick a different world. Finished worlds get a ✓.

Why squares?

  • a² + b² = c² is about areas: the squares on the two legs exactly fill the square on the hypotenuse. Watch the squares on the canyon change as you solve.
  • Each wrong choice is a common mistake, and each one has its own hint.
  • World 1 is one question per canyon: count the row of 1-ft squares along the rope and type the rope length c. Only the side lengths are shown - no areas yet.
  • Worlds 3 and 4 start with the formula already chosen. World 3 is harder: count the squares along one unlabeled side first, choose from four answers, type the rope length, and beat the fraying rope. World 4 finds a missing leg: the numbers are carved on a temple door, and solving opens it. World 5 is the summit: bigger triangles, and every step is a line of math with a blank to fill in.

    Teaching Notes for Educators

    Students swing across a jungle by solving the Pythagorean theorem one written step at a time. Every canyon is a right triangle made from the scene itself, so the numbers always describe something they can see.

    • The triangle: leg a (coral) is the distance straight across to the next platform, leg b (green) is how high the vine's anchor is above the explorer, and the hypotenuse c (blue) is the rope. The same three colors are used for every number on screen.
    • One step per swing: students choose the right line of the written solution - plug in, square, add (or subtract), take the square root - and each correct line swings them forward. Each wrong choice is a common mistake with its own hint, such as adding the sides, doubling instead of squaring, or forgetting the square root.
    • Why squares: a square is built on each side of the canyon triangle. As students work, the squares on the two legs fill with tiles and pour into the square on the rope, which they fill exactly - a visual proof of a² + b² = c².
    • Answers you can see: the big swing lands one rope length below the anchor, drawn to scale, so short ropes keep the explorer high in the trees and long ropes swing low. After the final step, a slow-motion close-up labels all three sides and reveals the missing length.
    • Five worlds: World 1 is the starting point: one question per canyon, where students count the 1-ft squares along the edge of the square on the rope and type its length - the hypotenuse, found by counting. World 2 walks through every step with 3-4-5 and 6-8-10 triangles and unit tiles. World 3 is the challenge round: one side of the triangle isn't labeled, so students first count the 1-ft grid squares along it, choose each step from four answers (every wrong one a common mistake), type the final rope length, and work against a fraying-rope timer (20 seconds per choice on the first problem, 3 seconds less on each problem after), with fewer hints and squares that stay "?" until their areas are found. World 4 finds a missing leg - the distance to the next platform - from numbers carved on a temple door. World 5 is the summit challenge: bigger triangles (6-8-10, 5-12-13, 9-12-15 and 8-15-17), a random mix of rope and distance problems, and no answer choices, with step hints and a perfect-squares list for support - each step is a line of the solution with one number blanked out, and students type what belongs there.
