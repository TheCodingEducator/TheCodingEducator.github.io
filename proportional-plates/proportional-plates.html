Proportional Plates

A fast-paced cooking game about proportions. You are the chef. Walk around the diner with the arrow keys (or click the floor). The kitchen is in the back-left corner and the tables are spread around the dining room. When a customer sits down and raises a hand, walk to their table and press Space to take the order, then walk back to the kitchen, where the order goes up on the ticket rail. Press Space at the stove to cook: read the recipe (or the market price), work out how much is needed for the size of the order, type the amount, and press Enter. Then carry the plate back to that customer, following the arrow and the glowing spot beside their table, and press Space to serve it. The food on the plate is sized by your answer. Customers arrive smiling and slowly get angrier the longer they wait; if one waits too long, they leave and post a 1-star review. A correct order makes the customer happy and earns a star. An answer within 25 percent upsets the customer, and one that is way off makes the customer furious; both earn a 1-star review, followed by a pop-up that shows the right method next to what you did, with a tape diagram. Three 1-star reviews close the restaurant. Levels: scaling a recipe up, scaling up and down, ratio tables, unit prices, and fractional scale factors. Practice mode has no waiting and no reviews. At the stove, the up and down arrow keys switch between orders and Escape steps away; otherwise Escape or the Menu button pauses.

Teaching Notes for Educators

Students run a diner as the chef: they take each order, carry it to the kitchen, scale the recipe (or work out the price) at the stove, and serve the plate. The customer's reaction shows how close the answer was.

  • How it plays: the arrow keys walk the chef and Space takes an order, cooks it, or serves it. Customers arrive smiling, and their faces slowly sour the longer they wait. A customer who waits too long walks out, so students have to think quickly as well as accurately. Practice mode removes the waiting and the reviews.
  • The math: in a proportional relationship every amount changes by the same factor. Find the scale factor (order ÷ recipe), or the amount for one serving or one item (the unit rate), then multiply.
  • Five levels: scale a recipe up, scale up and down, use ratio tables when the order isn't a whole multiple, find unit prices at the market, and finally fractional amounts and scale factors like 1½ and ⅔.
  • The additive mistake: when a student adds the difference (4 pancakes use 2 cups, so 12 pancakes use 10 cups), the pop-up names that exact mistake and shows why every amount has to grow by the same factor instead.
  • Tape diagrams: each wrong order is followed by the recipe drawn as equal blocks, the order as the same blocks repeated, and the student's amount as a bar beside the right length.
  • Real life: doubling a recipe, cooking for a crowd, comparing prices at the store, and converting a recipe that serves 4 into one that serves 6.
