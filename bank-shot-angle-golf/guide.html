Bank Shot: Angle Golf: Teacher Guide

Every hole of this mini golf course opens with an angle question. Students find the missing angle in a complementary or supplementary pair, then aim and putt. A correct answer sends the ball exactly where they aimed; a wrong one knocks it off course by the size of their error.

Standards

CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.7.G.B.5

Use facts about supplementary, complementary, vertical, and adjacent angles in a multi-step problem to write and solve simple equations for an unknown angle in a figure.

Key vocabulary

Words and phrases to use when you talk through the game with students. Each one appears in the game, its questions or its feedback.

  • angle: the figure formed by two rays that share an endpoint
  • degree (°): the unit for measuring angles; a full turn is 360°
  • right angle: an angle of exactly 90°, often marked with a small square
  • straight angle: an angle of exactly 180°; its sides form a straight line
  • complementary angles: two angles whose measures add to 90° (35° and 55°)
  • supplementary angles: two angles whose measures add to 180° (110° and 70°)
  • vertical angles: the angles across from each other where two lines cross; they are always equal

What students learn

Complementary angles add to 90°; supplementary angles add to 180°. A shot at a wall is a supplementary pair (the path and the wall make a straight line); a straight shot into open green is a complementary pair (a right angle with the aim line as one side). Other straight shots ask about vertical angles: the ball's path and a guide line cross at the ball, and the angle across from the given one is equal to it. Students solve for the angle that completes the pair. Because the answer controls the shot, precision matters: an answer that's 10° off sends the ball 10° off.

How the game works

  • Students aim a putt first, and the shot they choose becomes the question: a diagram with one known angle and one missing angle.
  • Bank shots off a wall use angles on a straight line (they add to 180°). Straight shots use a right angle (the parts add to 90°) or vertical angles (equal).
  • The typed angle sends the ball: a right answer plays the shot as aimed, and a wrong one sends it where that angle really points.
  • Golf Gamer eases in with angles in 10s, then 5s, then any whole number. Hole-In-One Hero shows three angles and, on the back nine, simple equations like (x + 5)°.

Worked examples

Supplementary bank shot (aiming at a wall): the ball's path and the wall make a straight line, and the shown angle is 35°.

35 + ? = 180 ? = 145°

Complementary straight shot (aiming at open green): the aim line is one side of a right angle, and the shown angle is 38°.

38 + ? = 90 ? = 52°

Hole-In-One Hero bank shot: the ball hits the wall at 35° and leaves at 35°. Find the angle between its path in and its path out.

35 + ? + 35 = 180 ? = 110°

Hole-In-One Hero equation: both bounce angles are 40°, and the missing angle is (x + 5)°.

180 - 40 - 40 = 100° x + 5 = 100 x = 95

Vertical angles (a straight shot): the ball's path and the guide line cross; the gold angle is 65°.

Angles across from each other are equal: ? = 65°

Common mistakes

Using the wrong total (90 instead of 180, or the other way round). The red wedge leaves a huge gap or spills far past the wall, and the sum card shows the total is off by 90°. The retry hint names the right total.

Subtraction slips with non-round angles like 90 − 37. Later holes use any whole number, so students can't rely on round numbers.

Stopping at the angle in Hero mode's equations. Students find the angle (100°) and type it instead of x, or undo the + 5 the wrong way (105). The hint names whichever one happened.

Picking the angle beside it on a vertical-angles question: 180° minus the gold angle instead of the angle across from it. Point out that vertical angles sit across the X from each other and are always equal.

Ways to use it in class

  • Printables. A free worksheet with an answer key and a two-to-a-page exit ticket match this game, ready to print or save as a PDF.
  • One round, then reflect. After nine holes, students write down which hole they missed and why, then replay the course to beat their score.
  • Predict first. Before hitting, students say whether the ball will bank off the wall or roll straight.
  • Pairs. One student solves, the other checks the arithmetic before the putt.
  • Exit ticket. "An angle is 47°. What is its complement? Its supplement?"

Discussion questions

  • Can an angle have a complement if it's bigger than 90°? A supplement?
  • Why does a ball leave a wall at the same angle it hit it?
  • If your answer was too small, where did the gray gap appear? What would fill it?
  • Why does a too-big answer spill past the wall?

Real-world connections

A golf ball, a billiard ball, a laser and a sound wave all leave a wall at the same angle they arrive. Complementary and supplementary angles also guide roof framing, carpentry, and any layout with corners.
