Math Billiards: Teacher Guide

A real top-down pool game where every shot is an angle problem. Students aim the cue anywhere they like, and the game draws the geometry of that very shot: a guide line, a cushion, or two parallel lines, with one angle marked and the missing one labeled x. The right x plays the shot exactly as aimed. A wrong x plays the shot that angle would really make, so students watch their mistake happen on the table.

Learning objectives

Students will:

  • use the fact that complementary angles add to 90° and supplementary angles (a linear pair) add to 180° to find an unknown angle;
  • recognize vertical angles where two lines cross and use the fact that they are equal;
  • identify corresponding, alternate interior, alternate exterior and same-side interior angles when parallel lines are cut by a transversal, and use whether they are equal or add to 180°;
  • write and solve a simple equation for the unknown angle (x + 145° = 180°), mentally, in one or two steps;
  • connect an angle measure to a direction: a different angle sends the ball somewhere else.

How the game works

Every shot follows the same loop: aim, see the geometry, solve for x, shoot.

  • Aim first. Students point the cue anywhere (mouse, trackpad, touch, or the arrow keys) and set the power by pulling the cue back. A short dotted preview shows the direction, never the whole path.
  • The geometry is the shot. The game zooms in and draws the angle problem on the actual shot: the shot's line, a guide line through the cue ball or the cushion where the shot lands, the right-angle box or parallel marks, one known angle and x. Every picture is checked before it appears: each label matches the drawing, and the answer gives back exactly the aimed direction.
  • Solve for x. Students type just the number (the degree sign is already there) and press Enter or Space.
  • Right answer: the shot plays exactly as aimed, and real pool physics decides the rest (collisions, cushions, pockets). Good math doesn't guarantee a good pool shot.
  • Wrong answer: the cue swings to the direction the wrong angle really makes, and that shot plays out (pockets shut). Then a comparison screen shows both answers, both paths (red for theirs, green for the right one), both angles drawn at the vertex, and the equation, like 55° + 145° = 200° ≠ 180°.
  • Same shot, more help. Students return to the exact same table and problem. Each further miss adds support: where to look, then the relationship's name and the equation, then the operation (x = 180° − 145°), then the full solution. Tries are unlimited, and there is no hint button to click before trying.

Difficulty

All four skills are available at every level; the difficulty changes the reasoning, not which math is allowed.

  • Beginner: one step, angles in multiples of 5°, every angle labeled and highlighted, and a plain-words reminder (“Look at the two angles on the straight line”).
  • Intermediate: any whole-number angle, more bank shots measured at the cushion (the angle with the cushion, or with the line straight out from it), and the relationship left for the student to recognize.
  • Challenge: two-step reasoning (vertical angles, then complementary; corresponding, then a straight line; complementary, then supplementary at the cushion), extra lines in the picture, and fewer marks.

Three modes

  • Practice: no rules and no opponent. A new random table appears after every shot. Pick any mix of the four skills.
  • Vs Computer: classroom 8-ball against the computer. The computer always gets the math right and shows its work step by step (the relationship, the equation, the answer), then visibly aims and shoots; it can still miss the pocket.
  • Two Players: classroom 8-ball for two students on one device. A wrong answer still plays out so the player sees the mistake, but every pocket is shut for that shot, and the turn passes.

Classroom 8-ball rules: red balls and blue balls are the two groups (blue balls also have a square number badge, so the groups differ by more than color); the first ball pocketed picks your color; pocket your color to keep shooting; the black 8-ball is last, and sinking it early loses. No called pockets, no fouls. If the cue ball drops, the turn passes and the next player places it anywhere.

Coins: each correct answer earns 1 coin, even if the shot misses. Coins buy cue sticks, table themes, ball styles and shot effects. Everything in the shop is just for looks. There are no points, rankings or leaderboards.

Worked examples

Complementary (Beginner): a right angle at the cue ball is split by the shot. One part is 55°.

x + 55° = 90° x = 90° − 55° = 35°

Supplementary at the cushion (Intermediate): the shot meets the cushion, making 128° with it on one side.

x + 128° = 180° x = 180° − 128° = 52°

Vertical (Beginner): the guide line crosses the shot's line at the cue ball. On the cue's side, the angle across from x is 40°.

Vertical angles are equal: x = 40°

Parallel lines (Intermediate): a guide through the cue ball is parallel to the cushion. Where the shot meets the cushion, the alternate interior angle is 133°.

Alternate interior angles are equal: x = 133°

Two steps (Challenge): perpendicular guide lines cross at the cue ball. On the cue's side, 49° is marked across from the part of x's right angle.

Vertical angles: the other part of the right angle = 49° x + 49° = 90° x = 41°

Common mistakes

Using 180 for a complementary pair (or 90 for a supplementary one). Look for the right-angle box: if it's there, the parts add to 90°. The game names this mistake when it happens.

Typing the given angle. When the two angles add up, x is the other part. The game points this out.

Treating vertical angles as supplementary. Vertical angles are equal; it's the angles next to each other on a line that add to 180°.

Mixing up alternate and same-side angles. Alternate angles are on opposite sides of the shot line and are equal; same-side interior angles add to 180°.

Key vocabulary

Words and phrases to use when you talk through the game with students. Each one appears in the game, its hints or its feedback.

  • complementary angles: two angles whose measures add to 90°
  • supplementary angles: two angles whose measures add to 180°
  • linear pair: two angles side by side that form a straight line; they are supplementary
  • vertical angles: the angles across from each other where two lines cross; they are equal
  • parallel lines: lines in the same plane that never meet
  • transversal: a line that crosses two or more other lines (here, the line of the shot)
  • corresponding angles: angles in the same position at each crossing; equal when the lines are parallel
  • alternate interior angles: between the parallel lines, on opposite sides of the transversal; equal
  • alternate exterior angles: outside the parallel lines, on opposite sides of the transversal; equal
  • same-side interior angles: between the parallel lines, on the same side of the transversal; they add to 180°

Ways to use it in class

  • Warm-up (10 minutes): Practice mode on Beginner with one skill, then add a second.
  • Station or partner work: Two Players mode. Partners must agree on x before the shooter submits; a wrong answer costs the turn.
  • Mistake talk: project one comparison screen and ask, “Why does 55° send the ball there?” The red and green paths make the point.
  • Exit data: the results screen shows questions attempted, first-try accuracy and a per-skill breakdown (80%+ strong, 40–79% growing, below 40% keep practicing). Ask students to record it, or use the printable exit ticket.

Discussion questions

  • Your answer was 20° too big. Which way did the ball go, and why?
  • When the shot meets the cushion, how are the angle with the cushion and the angle with the line straight out from the cushion related?
  • The computer solved it with “corresponding, then a straight line.” Could you solve it a different way?
  • Why does a correct answer not always sink the ball?

Real-world connections

Pool and billiards players use these exact relationships when they plan bank shots off the cushion. Surveyors, carpenters and road designers use parallel lines cut by a transversal and supplementary angles every day.

Access and privacy

No accounts, no logins, no names, and no data collected. Coins, cosmetics and settings are saved only in the student's own browser and are never sent anywhere. Plays with the mouse or trackpad, touch, or the keyboard alone (arrow keys to aim and set power, Enter or Space to shoot and to answer), works with screen readers (each problem is also described in words), and respects the device's reduced-motion setting. Sound can be turned off at any time.
